The photo shows a **KeyMouse** split keyboard/mouse hybrid. Instead of moving your hands between a keyboard and a mouse, each half is designed to be both at the same time.

Here's a breakdown of what I see:

### Overall layout

* Two **completely independent wired halves**, one for each hand.
* Each half has:

  * a **large palm rest** that supports almost your entire hand,
  * a **red thumb-operated trackball**,
  * a compact cluster of mechanical keys,
  * a few extra thumb and function buttons.

The idea is that your hands remain on the devices continuously—you type, move the pointer, and click without lifting them.

### Thumb controls

The thumbs rest naturally on the large red trackballs.

Each thumb can:

* move the mouse pointer,
* perform precise cursor movement,
* access nearby thumb buttons.

On the inside edge of each device there are several large modifier keys that are much easier to hit than ordinary keyboard keys.

### Finger keys

Each half contains roughly half of a keyboard.

The key clusters are angled inward toward the user, reducing wrist rotation.

The home rows appear to be:

**Left hand**

* Q O E U
* with surrounding letters such as A, L, K, X, Y, P, etc.

**Right hand**

* B D H G
* with surrounding letters such as W, T, C, N, S, L, R, etc.

The legends indicate a custom layout rather than a standard QWERTY arrangement.

### Function buttons

Near the center of each half is a vertical column of small round buttons.

These are labeled:

* F1–F6 on the left half
* F7–F12 on the right half

This keeps the function keys accessible without reaching across a full keyboard.

### Modifier keys

The outer thumb clusters contain large modifiers such as:

* Ctrl
* Esc
* Tab
* Enter
* Backspace
* Alt

These are positioned where the thumbs can reach them with very little movement.

### Ergonomics

Compared with a traditional keyboard:

* the hands stay shoulder-width apart,
* wrists remain nearly straight,
* almost no reaching is required,
* mouse movement is integrated directly under the thumbs,
* all frequently used keys are within a small radius of the resting finger positions.

### Construction

The devices appear to be:

* black 3D-printed housings (visible print-layer texture),
* fitted with mechanical key switches,
* connected by separate USB cables,
* fairly large palm rests that distribute the hand's weight.

### Musical Instrument Mapping

This device is designed to map all 12 chromatic notes per octave per hand.

**Right Hand (lowest to highest pitch):**
- Upper row: G → C → R → L
- Home row: H → T → N → S
- Lower row: M → W → V → Z

**Left Hand (lowest to highest pitch, mirror reflection):**
- Upper row: P → . → , → '
- Home row: U → E → O → A
- Lower row: ; → K → J → Q

Each hand controls a full chromatic octave (12 semitones). The left and right hands are mirror reflections of each other.

From our earlier discussion about turning this into a musical instrument, this hardware is actually well suited for that purpose because it naturally divides your controls into functional groups:

* **Thumbs:** continuous expressive controls (pitch bend, vibrato, modulation, navigation, octave shifting, etc.)
* **Home row:** the fastest musical actions (play notes, trigger scale degrees, intervals)
* **Upper/lower rows:** secondary musical functions (chords, harmony, articulation, effects)
* **Thumb modifier keys:** mode switching (Home, Scale, Harmony, Octave, Sustain, Layer, etc.)
* **Function buttons:** configuration, presets, recording, looping, transport controls.

The design minimizes hand movement, which is exactly what you'd want for an expressive instrument rather than simply mapping it to a conventional piano keyboard.
